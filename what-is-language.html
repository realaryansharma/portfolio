What Is Language, Really?

From sounds to talking to computers: a question about language itself, before Java, before programming, before computers.

Before we talk about Java, before we talk about programming, before we even talk about computers, I think we need to ask a much more fundamental question: what is language?

Because if you really think about it, language is one of the strangest things humans have ever created. A sound comes out of my mouth, and somehow another human being gets an idea inside their head. How?

I make a sound. You hear it. But the sound itself doesn't contain the idea. If I say "fire," there isn't some magical property inside that sound telling your brain, "hey, there is a hot chemical reaction happening over there." We've simply agreed that this particular collection of sounds represents this particular idea. And that is language.

At its most fundamental level, language is simply a medium through which one being transfers an idea to another being.

Once you look at language this way, things get interesting, because language doesn't have to be spoken. It can be sounds, gestures, symbols, drawings, mathematics. It can even be a sequence of lights flashing on and off. The medium doesn't matter. What matters is that we share the rules required to interpret it: a dictionary, a grammar, a protocol. And once two minds share a protocol, they can exchange information.

Knowledge that outlives the individual

Think about early humans. One person discovers that a particular plant is poisonous. With no language, that knowledge dies with them. But if they can communicate, they can tell another human: don't eat this. That person tells someone else. That person tells their children. Knowledge stops dying with the individual who discovered it. It survives. And it doesn't just survive, it accumulates. One generation learns something, the next starts from there and adds to it, and eventually you have something no individual could have built alone: a civilization.

This is where language gets genuinely strange, because humans didn't just use it to describe what was happening. We used it to talk about things that don't physically exist: gods, countries, money, stories, mathematics, laws, the future, the past, entire imaginary worlds. There's a concept for this called a meme, not the internet kind, the original idea: something that spreads from one mind to another and gets copied. A religion can spread. A survival technique can spread. A scientific theory can spread. Once information can move between minds, humans don't have to individually rediscover everything. We inherit it, and we build on it.

Something happens at location X. If I'm at location Y and I don't know about it, I can't react to it. But if that information reaches me quickly, I can make a different decision. This is basically what every communication technology has been doing throughout human history: speech, writing, letters, books, telegraphs, telephones, radio, television, and eventually the internet.

Think about someone like Charles Darwin. He develops an idea, writes it down, publishes it, and someone somewhere else reads it. But information still has physical limitations. A book has to be printed. A letter has to travel. A person has to physically move. Information can only move as fast as the medium carrying it. Then the internet collapsed that distance almost entirely. I can create something here in India, and someone on the other side of the planet can access it almost immediately.

A different kind of listener

But then something more interesting happened. Humans didn't just build a system for communicating with other humans. We built something we could communicate with: a machine. A computer.

Think about what a computer really is from this angle. It stores information, processes information, follows instructions, performs tasks. But there's a problem. If I want to collaborate with another human, I can speak English. If I want to collaborate with a computer, I can't walk up to it and say, "hey, calculate this for me." Maybe one day I can. But fundamentally, the machine needs some way of interpreting what I'm telling it. So we need another language. And this is where the journey into computer science actually begins.

Because before Java, there was programming. Before programming, there were programming languages. Before programming languages, there were machines. And before machines could understand us, we had to answer something much more basic: how does a machine understand anything at all?

How do you go from electricity, to switches, to bits, to logic, to instructions, to programs, to operating systems, to a graphical interface, to the browser you're probably reading this on, and eventually to something like Java? That's the journey worth taking. Not just "here is how Java works," but why Java exists at all. Why programming languages exist. Why computers use binary. Why electricity can represent information. How a few billion tiny switches eventually becomes something that can run a game, process a payment, render a video, or talk to you through an AI.

Because when you zoom out far enough, computer science starts looking less like a collection of technologies and more like one long story about communication. Humans learned to communicate with humans. Then we built machines. And eventually, we had to learn how to communicate with the machines too.

So: how do we actually talk to a computer? That's the next dispatch.
