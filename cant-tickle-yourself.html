You Can't Tickle Yourself

Why you cannot tickle yourself, why a pulled chair feels like falling, and what a prediction error has to do with training AI.

Try tickling yourself. Go on. Try it.

Nothing happens, right? But when someone else does it, you cannot stop laughing. Why?

Because your brain already knew

Your brain knew exactly what you were about to do. It predicted every move, so there was no surprise left to feel. And this is not just a tickling thing. It is how your brain experiences almost everything.

The chair

Think about sitting down on a chair. Before you even touch it, your brain already knows how far you will drop, how the room will shift in your eyes, and how the seat will feel when you land. You have done it a thousand times, so it does not wait for reality. It predicts it.

Now imagine someone pulls that chair away from behind you. Your stomach drops. Your heart jumps. Every bit of your attention goes to one thing: falling.

Why? Because, for once, your brain's prediction was wrong.

Prediction error

That gap between what your brain expected and what actually happened is called a prediction error. Your brain uses it to update its model, so that next time it is a little less surprised.

Predict. Observe. Measure the difference. Update. That is predictive coding.

The engineer in me

Sound familiar? It is the same shape of loop we use to train AI. Guess. Check. Calculate the error. Adjust. Run it enough times and the guesses get good, and the errors get small.

I find this oddly comforting. The thing in your skull and the thing in a data center are both just trying to be less wrong about what comes next.

"You don't live in reality. You live in your brain's best guess of it."

Aryan Sharma

So the next time something surprises you, notice it. That is your brain learning, in real time.

Margin note

Predict, compare, update. It is a training loop before it is a theory of the mind, and I work with loops like this for a living.
